Exploring the Chemistry
of Stars and Planets

My research focuses on measuring the chemical compositions of cool, low-mass stars (M dwarfs) and exploring what their chemistry can tell us about the planets that form around them. Using high-resolution near-infrared spectroscopy, I develop methods to measure elements important to planetary interiors and atmospheres, then test how reliably those measurements can be recovered.

  • Connecting Stellar Chemistry to Planet Formation

    What can the chemical composition of a star tell us about the planets orbiting it? M dwarfs are among the most common stars in our Galaxy and host many known small exoplanets. Measuring their elemental abundances can help us investigate the materials available when planets form, from the iron and silicates of rocky interiors to carbon- and oxygen-bearing compounds.

    I am developing a framework for determining individual elemental abundances from high-resolution near-infrared spectra of M dwarfs. Using observations from the SPIRou spectrograph and synthetic spectra generated with MARCS stellar atmospheres and Turbospectrum, I identify and fit atomic and molecular absorption features. This work includes careful line selection, spectral normalization, and assessment of uncertainties, all especially challenging in the crowded spectra of cool stars.

    I am applying this approach to nearby M dwarfs, including stars with known planets, to investigate abundance patterns and ratios such as C/O and Mg/Si. These measurements offer a way to explore possible links between host-star chemistry and planetary composition, while recognizing that a planet's final composition also depends on the complex processes of its formation and evolution.

  • Testing M-Dwarf Abundances with Binary Stars

    Measuring chemical abundances in M dwarfs is difficult because their spectra contain many overlapping atomic and molecular features. To test and improve the accuracy of our measurements, I am developing an empirical calibration using wide binary systems that contain both a Sun-like FGK star and an M dwarf.

    Stars in such systems are expected to have formed from the same material and therefore to share very similar initial chemical compositions. Since FGK-star abundances are generally easier to measure, they provide a valuable reference against which to compare the abundances derived independently for their M-dwarf companions.

    Our program has obtained high-resolution near-infrared SPIRou spectra of 24 FGK–M binary systems. By comparing the two stars in each pair, we aim to identify systematic trends and calibrate M-dwarf elemental abundances across different stellar temperatures and metallicities. The goal is to make M-dwarf chemical measurements more robust for future studies of exoplanet host stars.

  • Cygnus Loop: Radio Structure and Magnetic Fields

    During 2021–2022, I studied the Cygnus Loop, a well-known supernova remnant with an unusual shape: a roughly circular northern structure and an extended southern region. One possible explanation is that the original explosion expanded into a lower-density region; another is that the structure involves two overlapping supernova remnants.

    I investigated the remnant using radio observations, where relativistic electrons emit synchrotron radiation in magnetic fields. I produced maps of its spectral index and Faraday rotation measure to look for differences in the emitting particles and magnetic-field properties across the structure. These measurements provide clues to the remnant's morphology and evolution.
